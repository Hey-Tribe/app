How to Prepare for a Parent-Teacher Conference

October 18, 20264 min read

A conference is often only 10 to 20 minutes, so a little preparation helps you use it well. This guide covers what to gather, what to ask and what to do afterward.

Conference slots are short, and the time goes quickly. Parents often leave realizing they forgot to ask the one thing they meant to raise. A page of notes made ahead of time fixes most of that.

The steps below take about 30 minutes in total, spread over a few days.

Gather what you already have

Start with the evidence you have at home. Look at recent report cards, graded work, teacher emails and any notes your child has mentioned. Put them in one folder so you can find them quickly.

  • The latest report card or progress report.
  • Two or three samples of recent work, one you are proud of and one that was hard.
  • A list of dates you want to mention, such as absences or changes at home.
  • Your notes and your child’s answers to the prompts below.

Check the school’s own guidance too. Some schools send a pre-conference form or a list of topics. Read it before you write your own questions.

Ask your child first

Children often know things the grade book does not show. A few easy prompts:

  • “What is going well at school right now?”
  • “What is the hardest part of the day?”
  • “Is there anything you want me to tell your teacher?”
  • “Who do you sit with at lunch?”

Write the answers down. Older children can join the conference in some schools, so ask the teacher if that is an option.

Write three questions

Limit yourself to three main questions. More than that, and the meeting turns into a rush. Pick the ones that will change what you do at home. Some examples:

  1. What does a typical day look like for my child in class?
  2. Where would you like to see the most growth by the next report?
  3. What can we do at home that would help?

If you have a specific worry, such as reading pace or friendship troubles, say so plainly in the first minute. Teachers can plan the time around it.

For older children, add one question about independence, such as “Is my child speaking up when they need help?” Teachers see how children act when parents are not there, and that is useful to hear.

Plan the logistics

Confirm the time, the room and whether the meeting is in person or by video. If two parents are attending, decide who takes notes. If you need childcare, book it early, because slots near the school day fill fast.

Leave a buffer of 15 minutes on either side. Running late takes a bite out of an already short meeting.

Take notes during the meeting

Use a simple three-line format for each topic.

TopicWhat the teacher saidWhat we will do
ReadingStrong with stories, slower with instructionsRead directions aloud together on Tuesdays
MathNeeds practice with timed testsTen minutes of flash cards, three nights a week

The example above is only an illustration. End the meeting by repeating back your main takeaways, which gives the teacher a chance to correct anything.

Follow up within two days

Send a brief thank-you email that lists what you agreed on. Share the plan with your child in plain terms, starting with something positive. Put one or two actions on the calendar, with a date to check progress, such as four weeks later.

Sample email: “Thank you for meeting with us today. We agreed to read directions aloud together at home and to check in again in four weeks. Please let us know if anything changes before then.”

If the teacher raised a concern about learning or health, ask the school about the right next step and follow their guidance. A conference is a starting point for a plan.

How HeyTribe helps

The conference can go on the calendar as a plan, with a lane for each parent and a note about who is going. The School hub keeps one tab per child, which makes it easy to find forms and due dates before the meeting. You can use any notebook or app for your notes, and paper works fine.

A closing tip

Bring a pen and leave your phone in your bag. The teacher notices, and so does your child when you describe the meeting later.
