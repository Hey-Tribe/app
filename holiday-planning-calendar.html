Holidays

A Holiday Planning Calendar for Families

October 18, 20263 min read

The holiday season goes better when the big decisions happen early and the small tasks are spread over many weeks. Here is a calendar you can adapt to any holiday.

Holidays collect many small jobs: gifts, meals, travel, cards, school events and extended family. The pressure comes from all of them landing in the same two weeks.

A planning calendar spreads the work out. It also gives you time to say no to things that do not fit. Use the schedule below for any holiday, and move the dates to match yours.

Decide the big things first

About two to three months before, make the decisions that others depend on. Where will you spend the day? Who is hosting? Are you traveling, or are people coming to you? When extended family is involved, early answers prevent tangled plans later.

Talk with your partner first and agree on what you want. Then contact relatives with a clear offer. “We would like to come on Saturday and leave Sunday morning” is easier to answer than “What are we doing this year?”

Set a budget and a gift plan

Write down a total amount for gifts, food, travel and extras. Divide it into lines so you can see where it goes. Many families find it works to give each child a few gifts, such as something to want, something to need, something to wear and something to read.

  • List every person you buy for, with an amount beside each name
  • Keep a running note of ideas all year long
  • Agree with relatives on limits or a name draw if the group is large
  • Set a shipping cutoff date on your calendar

For help turning a plan into a routine, see our guide on the monthly family planning session.

4 weeksWrite and send cards, plan the menu, check school and community event dates
2 weeksBuy food that keeps, wrap gifts in short sessions, clean the guest space
1 weekShop for fresh food, set the cooking schedule, pack bags
The day beforeDo the prep that can be done ahead and go to bed on time

Share the work

Hand out jobs in a way that matches ages. Young children can make cards or help wrap. Older kids can set the table, peel vegetables, or write a thank-you note. Adults divide the larger jobs, and each person keeps the same jobs year after year so no one has to ask.

It helps to write down who does what. A short list on the fridge removes the quiet resentment that builds when one person carries it all. Our piece on the mental load explains why this matters.

Protect the schedule

Plan for rest. After each big event, leave a quiet block with no visits. Keep regular bedtimes where you can, since overtired children can make the day harder for everyone.

Say no to some invitations. If your calendar already feels full, read our guide on a family calendar that is too full for ways to decide.

Keep a notes page for next year

Memory fades quickly after a busy season, so capture details while they are fresh.

When the holiday is over, write a short list: what worked, what you would skip, and what each person liked. Add the names of gift ideas that were a hit and the quantities of food that were eaten. Note the dates when shipping slowed down, which stores sold out, and how long the cooking really took. Next year you can copy the plan and change only what needs changing, which saves an evening of thinking.

How HeyTribe helps

One last tip

Pick one thing to drop this year. A smaller holiday that everyone enjoys beats a long one that everyone survives.
