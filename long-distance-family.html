Staying Connected With Long-Distance Family

October 18, 20263 min read

Distance makes closeness take some planning. These ideas help children and relatives keep real relationships through calls, mail, shared projects and well-planned visits.

When grandparents, aunts and cousins live far away, relationships can fade if nobody tends them. The good news is that children form bonds with faraway relatives when contact is regular and easy.

The plan here favors small, repeated contact over big occasional events. It works for any distance and any age.

Set a regular call

Pick a fixed time each week or every two weeks and put it on both calendars. A predictable slot beats a vague promise to call soon. Check time zones first, and choose an hour that suits the youngest child, since a call during a nap or a meltdown goes poorly.

Keep the calls short for young children. Ten minutes is plenty for a toddler. Older kids may talk longer, especially if they have something to show.

Make calls easier for little ones

Small children find screens hard to follow. A few things help.

  • Prop the phone or tablet so both hands are free
  • Do an activity together: reading a book, singing, or drawing
  • Let the child show a toy, a drawing or a pet
  • Ask the relative to read a bedtime story on a call
  • Keep a short list of questions, such as “What did you eat today?”

Relatives can ask a child to pick the topic. Children usually have plenty to say once they are asked about their own interests.

Share the small moments

Send photos and short videos during the week. A shared album means relatives see ordinary days as well as holidays. Ask them to send the same back, so children see their grandparents’ home, garden or dog.

Agree who sends what and how often. A few messages each week is enough. Too many can become a burden, and too few leaves gaps.

Use the mail

Children love getting something in the mail. Cards, drawings, a postcard, or a small package can sit on the fridge for weeks. Set a monthly habit of sending a drawing, and ask relatives to write back. Older kids can keep a pen pal exchange with a cousin.

Do something together at a distance

Pick a shared activity that goes on over time.

  1. Read the same book and talk about each chapter
  2. Play an online or board game on a call
  3. Cook the same recipe on the same day and compare
  4. Grow a plant from the same seeds and share photos
  5. Record a story or a song and send it as a message

Share the names of friends, teachers and favorite things before a call, so a grandparent can ask about them. A short list on a shared note works. Relatives who know the details can ask better questions, and children feel known.

Plan visits ahead

When you can visit, put the dates on the calendar early, and decide who travels in which direction. Keep expectations realistic. A few quiet days with no packed schedule often means more than a whirlwind of outings. Our family vacation planning guide covers the booking order, and the holiday planning calendar helps when visits tie to holidays.

Check official sources for travel documents and any health rules if the trip crosses a border. See our guide on keeping track of travel documents.

Busy seasons will break the pattern, and so will illness, school pressure and time zone shifts. When that happens, skip a call without guilt and send a short voice message. A small gesture keeps the thread alive until the schedule returns.

One last tip

Ask the children what they want to do with their faraway relatives. Their ideas are often simpler and better than the adults’.
