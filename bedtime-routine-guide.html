How to Build a Bedtime Routine That Lasts

October 18, 20264 min read

A good bedtime routine is short, predictable and the same most nights. This guide shows how to set one up, how to handle stalling, and how to keep it going when life gets busy.

Bedtime is easier when the steps are the same every night and the child knows what is coming. Keep it short and plain, so either parent, a sitter or a grandparent can repeat it without a long explanation.

The advice below is general. Sleep needs vary by child and by age, so follow your pediatrician’s guidance on how much sleep your child needs.

Start with a target lights-out time

Choose the time the light goes off, then work backward. Many families land on a routine of 20 to 40 minutes. If lights out is 8:00, the routine starts at about 7:20. Write the start time down, because it is the part that tends to drift.

Check the time against the rest of your evening. If dinner ends at 6:45 and the dishes take until 7:15, the routine will always start late. Move dinner or move bedtime, but make the numbers fit.

A sample sequence

Here is an example for a school-age child. Shorten it for younger children.

  1. 7:20, tidy up. Five minutes to put toys or school things away.
  2. 7:25, bath or wash. Not every night if that does not suit your family.
  3. 7:40, pajamas and teeth. Same order each time.
  4. 7:50, book or quiet talk. One or two books, or ten minutes of reading alone.
  5. 8:00, lights out. A hug, a phrase you always say, door open or closed as the child likes.

The final step matters most. A short, fixed goodnight phrase tells the child the day is done.

Cut the screens before the routine

Decide where screens stop. Many families end them before the bath or before pajamas, so the wind-down is already quiet when the routine begins. The screen time schedule guide covers how to set that boundary and keep it.

Handle stalling in advance

Most children try for one more drink, one more story, one more question. Deal with these before they start:

  • Offer water and a bathroom trip as part of the routine, so they cannot ask later.
  • Agree on a limit for books, such as two, and keep to it.
  • Give a single “one more thing” allowance, like one extra hug or one question.
  • If a child gets up, walk them back calmly with as little talk as possible.

Boring is good. The less interesting the response, the less reward there is in stalling.

Share the work between adults

If one parent always does bedtime, that parent is stuck, and the child may only accept one person. Try alternating nights, or split the routine: one adult does bath and pajamas, the other does books and lights out. A short written list helps a sitter or grandparent follow the same steps. Keep it to a few lines: the order, any special toy, the goodnight phrase, and who to call.

For babies and very young toddlers, the details differ. See daily routines for toddlers for those.

Keep the room itself calm. Dim the lights in the last half hour, keep the noise down in the rest of the house where you can, and have a favorite toy or blanket within reach so nobody is hunting for it at 7:58. A night light or a glass of water by the bed can head off a call for help later.

Keep it going on busy nights

Late practices, visitors and travel will push things around. Rather than dropping the routine, use a short version: pajamas, teeth, one book, lights out, in about 10 minutes. The order stays the same even when the length changes. After a disrupted night or week, go back to the normal routine the next day without making it a big event.
