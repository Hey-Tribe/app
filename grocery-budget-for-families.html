Grocery budget

Cutting the Family Grocery Bill Without Cutting Meals

October 18, 20264 min read

Groceries are one of the few big household costs you can change in a single week. A few steady habits usually save more than any coupon trick.

When money feels tight, the grocery budget is often the first thing people look at, because it is flexible. It is also easy to cut in ways that make everyone miserable. The aim here is to spend less while the family still eats well.

The ideas below are about habits and planning. Your actual numbers depend on where you live, your family size and your needs, so use the examples as shapes you can fill in.

Find out what you spend now

Before you change anything, track your spending for four weeks. Add up grocery stores, takeout and the quick convenience stops. Many families find that the small extra trips add up to more than they guessed.

Divide the total by four to get a weekly average. That number is your starting point. Next, set a target that is a little lower, perhaps 10 percent less, and try it for a month. A gentle target is easier to keep than a dramatic one.

Plan meals around what you have

Look in the fridge, freezer and pantry before you write the list. Build two or three dinners around items that need using. This one step can shrink a shopping trip noticeably.

Our guide to planning a week of dinners covers a quick way to do it. Plan five dinners and leave two nights open for leftovers, so you do not buy for meals that never happen.

Shop with a list and a single trip

Every extra trip to the store means extra items in the cart. Do one main shop a week, and make a short top-up trip only for fresh produce or milk if you must.

  • Write the list by store section so you do not wander.
  • Eat before you go, or send someone else if you are hungry and tired.
  • Take older children and give them a job, such as finding the cheapest pasta.
  • Leave off anything that is not on the list unless you can name the meal it belongs to.

Compare unit prices

The big number on the shelf tag is the package price. The small number is the price per ounce, pound or count. Compare the small number. The larger package is not always cheaper, and the store brand is often the same food for less.

Try store brands for basics like flour, canned tomatoes, beans, rice and frozen vegetables. If your family notices a difference, go back to the brand you like for that item and keep the savings elsewhere.

Where the savings usually come from

HabitWhy it helps
Meatless meals once or twice a weekBeans, lentils and eggs usually cost less per serving
Cooking larger batchesOne cooking effort covers two dinners
Packing lunches and snacksReplaces many small purchases
Frozen fruit and vegetablesLess spoilage, easy to portion
Planned leftoversFewer takeout nights

Waste less of what you buy

Throwing away food is throwing away money. Keep a small shelf or bin of items to use first, and check it before each shopping trip. Freeze bread, bananas and cooked portions before they go bad. If you want more tactics, read the guide on cutting food waste at home.

Bulk buying saves money only when you finish the food. A giant bag of something your family eats slowly can cost more in the end if half of it goes stale. Buy large sizes of items you use every week, such as rice, oats or frozen chicken, and small sizes of everything else.

Snacks and drinks are another quiet drain. Single-serve packs cost more per ounce than the large bag. Portion the big bag into small containers on Sunday and you keep the convenience at a lower price.

Finally, watch the end of the month. Many families find that the last week is when the budget runs dry and the takeout rises. Keep a few cheap meals on hand for those days, like pasta and sauce or rice and beans, and the pressure drops.

Review once a month

At the end of each month, compare what you spent with your target and note one change to try next. Keep what worked and drop what made dinner worse for everyone.
